Clock Work
Who owns the hours?

Platform work in India is sold on flexible hours. This exhibit asks what the platforms keep when they hand over the choice of hours — and who pays for what they keep.

Primary image

Eight O'Clock, Park Street

I counted nineteen of them outside the restaurant cluster, and not one was sitting down. The bags were stacked against the railing — orange, green, a few unbranded — while the riders stood in the road with their phones held close to their faces, screens lighting them from below. Two had helmets still on. Nobody was talking much. Every few minutes one would look up, swing a leg over a scooter and go, and within a minute someone else would arrive to take the gap. The queue never got shorter. It was ten past eight on a Thursday.

Every rider in the frame is a man.

The hour is the whole point. Evening is when the orders arrive, and the platforms pay accordingly — higher per-order rates during the dinner rush, bonus slabs that unlock at a certain number of deliveries, supplements conditional on staying logged in through the window. Miss the window and you haven't just worked fewer hours. You've worked the cheap ones. This is what I keep returning to about the promise of flexible work: the choice on offer is when to work, but the platform keeps the decision about which hours are worth anything. A mother who logs off at seven to be home for dinner is not trading hours for autonomy. She is taking the discount rate.

And some platforms have gone further, restricting women from delivering after dark on grounds of safety. The concern is real — I am not going to pretend a woman alone on a scooter at eleven at night faces what these men face. But look at what the policy does. It removes women from precisely the hours in which the money is concentrated, and it does so in the language of protection. Earnings and safety are placed on opposite sides, and women are permitted to choose only one.

This is where Shilpa Phadke's argument in Why Loiter? does work that a wage analysis cannot. Phadke's claim is that the discourse of safety functions to police women's access to public space rather than to make that space safe. The response to risk is always restriction, and restriction arrives dressed as care. Women are granted the streets conditionally — while commuting, while shopping, while purposeful — but never simply to be present without justification. Loitering is the freedom withheld.

Look again at the photograph. Nineteen men loitering, and being paid a premium for it. Their work in that moment is not delivery; it is waiting, visible, available on a street corner at night. Standing around is the job. And standing around after dark, without errand or explanation, is the one thing the city has never extended to women.

The platform does not need to pay women less. It only needs to pay extra for a posture women aren't allowed to hold.

Reading referenced

Shilpa Phadke, Why Loiter? Women and Risk on Mumbai Streets — on safety discourse as a restriction on women's access to public space, and loitering as the withheld freedom.
